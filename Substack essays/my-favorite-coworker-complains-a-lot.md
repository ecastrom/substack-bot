# My Favorite Coworker Complains a Lot

**Why the person who names the problem out loud is doing unpaid public work — and why teams without one are quietly in trouble.**

My favorite coworker complains a lot.

I have been working in Mexico for the last six months. Before that, I spent a long stay at George Mason University's Korea campus, where my working life was, in many ways, a solitary one. One of the things I most looked forward to on arriving here was the simplest thing I had been missing: company at lunch. A table, an hour, and a conversation that could drift from economics to politics to the small daily frictions of being expats somewhere you are still learning to live. It turns out that for an economist, this is not just pleasant. It is how a lot of the real thinking actually gets done.

My favorite coworker comes to those lunches with a particular gift. He notices things. The form we have to fill out three times. The committee that meets to decide what the next committee should decide. The rule that looks coherent on paper and falls apart on first contact with a real human being. The course-evaluation process that is measuring something nobody can quite name. Some of this he complains about for sport, and I enjoy it for the same reason. But most of it he complains about because he wants to fix it — and at some point, usually a few minutes in, the venting quietly turns into design. What institutional arrangement would make this better? What would a department that took the problem seriously actually do? If we were running the place, what would be the first thing we changed?

There is a particular freedom in complaining as an expat among expats. You are not locked into the defensive habits of people who have spent a whole career inside a single institution. You notice the peculiarities because you have seen something else. You are willing to name them because your reputation does not entirely depend on the people you are describing. We compare notes. In Korea, X worked like this. In the United States, Y looked that way. In Mexico, here is the specific thing that keeps surprising me. None of this is a ranking of countries — the expat tourism of "my home country did it better" is the least interesting move on the table. It is raw material for a shared exercise: why is this particular arrangement the way it is, and what would actually be gained by changing it?

My favorite coworker is, technically, my boss. We co-author several projects and work in collaboration — arguments settled on the merits, not by rank — which is part of why the complaining works at all. What makes him useful, and not merely abrasive, is that every complaint is two things at once: *specific* (about *this* form, *this* estimator, *this* paragraph in a draft) and *constructive* (here is what would fix it, or at least a sharper version of the problem). People tend to think of someone like this as a personality trait to be tolerated. I have come to think of him as one of the reasons our work — and our lunches — are better than they would otherwise be.

And here is the broader claim this essay is about: complaining, properly understood, is one of the most underrated social technologies we have.

## Exit, voice, and the information silent people withhold

Fifty-six years ago, Albert Hirschman published a book — *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty*. When an organization encounters a problem, he argued, its members have two broad responses. They can **exit** (leave, switch supplier, quit the project, emigrate), or they can use **voice** (complain, protest, propose reform). A third variable, loyalty, determines how long they stay and invest in voice before giving up.

Hirschman's key point was that voice carries information that exit cannot. When a dissatisfied customer silently switches brands, the firm loses revenue but learns nothing about *why*. When she complains first, the firm learns what is broken — and has a chance to fix it. The same logic applies to employees, citizens, patients, students, and coauthors.

This is the argument for the complainer in one sentence: **they are the channel through which reality reaches the decision-maker.** Without them, the decision-maker just loses customers, coauthors, employees, and voters, without ever knowing what went wrong.

## What silence costs

The organizational case for voice was built, oddly, in hospitals. When Amy Edmondson started studying medical errors in the 1990s, she found something counterintuitive: the best-performing nursing units reported *more* errors, not fewer. They were not making more mistakes. They felt safe enough to name the mistakes they made. The worse units made the same errors again and again, in silence.

Three decades of research since has converged on the same finding: teams where it is safe to speak up learn faster, innovate more, and retain better people. The mechanism is almost embarrassingly simple. Frontline workers — nurses, assembly-line operators, junior coauthors — hold private knowledge about what is actually happening that the people in charge do not have. Voice is the pipeline that moves that knowledge upward. Without it, the org chart is flying blind.

The counterfactual is visible in almost every major organizational disaster of the last forty years. Boeing's 737 MAX: internal engineering warnings ignored. The Mid Staffordshire NHS scandal: staff and patient complaints suppressed for years, hundreds of avoidable deaths, a 2013 public inquiry that is painful to read. Challenger and Columbia: known concerns, blocked channels. These are not stories of missing information. They are stories of silenced voice.

Commercial aviation learned the lesson earlier than most industries and is now built around it. After the 1977 Tenerife disaster — still the deadliest accident in aviation history — the industry introduced Crew Resource Management, a training regime whose central purpose is to teach junior officers to challenge captains. NASA runs a confidential near-miss reporting system for pilots that has collected more than two million reports since 1976. The Toyota Production System includes an *andon* cord that any assembly-line worker can pull to halt production — a piece of hardware whose real function is to convert a single worker's complaint into an immediate organizational response.

Notice: none of these are technologies. They are rules about who is allowed to complain and who must listen.

## Complaint as a public good

Economists call something a public good when my use of it does not diminish yours and no one can be fenced out. Complaining is not a textbook public good, but it shares the structural feature that matters: **the person who complains pays the cost; everyone else collects the benefit.** The complainer spends time, political capital, and sometimes a relationship. The better product, the safer hospital, the cleaner government, the less embarrassing paragraph in the paper — those flow to everyone, including the people who stayed silent.

This is the free-rider structure Mancur Olson described sixty years ago in *The Logic of Collective Action*. Left alone, it produces too little voice. Most people, most of the time, choose the cheap option: stay quiet, let the problem slide, let someone else notice. The person who reliably pays the private cost of naming the problem is subsidizing everyone around them.

Which is why a single reliable complainer on a team is so valuable — and why a team with *zero* complainers is quietly in trouble.

Institutions that want the benefits of voice have to subsidize it somehow. Whistleblower laws that pay *qui tam* rewards under the U.S. False Claims Act do this explicitly: they convert a public good into a private incentive, and they have recovered more than USD 85 billion in government fraud cases since 1986. Ombudsman offices, anonymous reporting lines, consumer-protection agencies, and corporate speak-up programs are lower-powered versions of the same idea. Norms that treat the complainer as a contributor rather than a nuisance are the cheapest technology of the lot — and the hardest to install.

## A short cultural aside, handled carefully

I have lived in Colombia, the United States, South Korea, and Mexico, and my subjective impression is that the United States and South Korea tolerate open complaint more readily than most Latin American contexts, where the complainer risks being labeled *quejetas* or *conflictivo* — a reputational sanction that falls, anecdotally, hardest on women and on people with less status. Four countries are not a dataset. But the broad pattern is recognizable in the comparative marketing literature: consumers in high-power-distance and collectivist contexts more often substitute silent exit and word-of-mouth for direct complaint to firms.

South Korea is the interesting case that breaks the simple East–West binary. Korean civic culture combines strong in-group deference with famously assertive public protest and viral online complaint. The *gapjil* discourse — in which abuses of power by superiors are named in public — is itself a cultural technology for lowering the cost of voice.

The point isn't to rank cultures. It is that the price of voice is a policy variable — set by institutions, managers, and norms — and lowering it has public returns.

## Not all complaint is useful

There is a real distinction between **constructive voice** — specific, addressed to someone who can act, usually with a proposal attached — and chronic, unfocused negativity that loads costs on listeners without transmitting usable information. Organizational researchers treat these as different constructs with different correlates, and they are right to. Social media has made the destructive variant cheaper to produce and more contagious. Platforms that reward outrage confuse the signal.

But the correct response is not to suppress complaint broadly. It is to build channels, norms, and relationships that reward the constructive kind.

The failure mode of a speak-up culture is noise. The failure mode of a silence culture is catastrophe. The asymmetry matters, and it points in one direction.

## Back to the coworker

Which brings me back to the complainer in my working life. He is, by the standards of the literature, almost a textbook case of constructive voice: specific, actionable, directed at the person who can do something about the problem, usually with a proposed fix attached. The fact that he is technically senior and still chooses complaint over quiet authority — that he names problems out loud rather than silently deciding — is itself a small act of institutional generosity. It lets a more junior coauthor push back. It makes the norm symmetrical.

The next time a complainer annoys you — at a store counter, in a faculty meeting, in the group chat for a paper — it is worth remembering that they are doing unpaid informational work on behalf of everyone else in the room. They will bear most of the cost. The rest of us, silent and comfortable, will collect most of the benefit.

That is not weakness of character. Very often, that is a public good in action.

I am glad I get to work with one.
