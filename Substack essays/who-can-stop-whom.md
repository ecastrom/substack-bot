# Who can stop whom?

**Why the stupidest moment to weaken separation of powers is when you like the president.**

Several weeks ago I had lunch with a couple of Peruvian colleagues. The conversation drifted, as it does among economists, from data to gossip to whatever weirdness one of us had run into that week. At some point we landed on Peru. Peru comes up often in my work and in my conversations. My boss is Peruvian. So was my PhD advisor. So is my research assistant. At some point, Peru stops being a case study and becomes part of the furniture of my intellectual life.

Peru has had eight presidents in the last ten years, a real oddity even by Latin American standards — and Latin America is the region that has tried almost every form of government. The most recent removal happened in February 2026. One of my colleagues offered a hypothesis about that impeachment that stayed with me. Congress did not really want to remove the president, he argued. Not fully. The vote was performative, a political signal before the next election, a way for legislators to show loyalty to their platform. Theater. Then the theater became reality. A former president's ally was named interim president, partly to avoid stirring the mud even more. The country woke up with a new government because its politicians had grown accustomed to playing with loaded institutional weapons.

Peru is not the worst case in Latin America. It is not. But Peru is the case that punishes the simple version of every argument about democracy. The simple version goes: presidents bad, congress good; executives bad, courts good; or, more generally, separation of powers is just constraints, and constraints are just safeguards. Peru is what happens when the constraints exist and the politicians do not internalize any cost for using them. It is the case that made me see something I had stopped seeing — that the most important question in a democracy is not *who wins?* but *who can stop whom?*

I wanted to write an essay about separation of powers since that lunch. I did not. Papers have a way of disciplining moral urgency, and I had a field experiment to run. What pushed me back to it was Colombia, my country of origin. We have presidential elections in ten days. The incumbent president, Gustavo Petro, and the front-runner to succeed him, Iván Cepeda, are both actively pursuing a constituent assembly (*asamblea constituyente*) — a body with the power to rewrite the constitution.[^cepeda-firmas]

## The loser's power

Antonin Scalia [once made a point](https://x.com/i/status/2025152903921475665) that has aged disturbingly well. Americans, he said, often think their freedoms come from the Bill of Rights. But many authoritarian constitutions also contain beautiful promises. The Soviet Constitution had rights on paper. So do many regimes nobody would confuse with free societies. The difference is not the poetry of the legal text. The difference is the structure of power.

The most important democratic institution is not the election. It is the loser's ability to still have power.

Congress must still matter when the president is popular. Courts must still matter when the majority is angry. Central banks must still matter when politicians want cheap credit. Local governments must still matter when the national government wants uniform obedience. The opposition must still matter after losing.

That is what separation of powers is for. In the same conversation about rights on paper, Scalia said something else: *Americans need to learn to love the gridlock.* The branches were never supposed to agree easily. The whole point of the architecture is that they slow each other down, that no one of them can simply move when it wants to. The frustration is the point.

People say they love democracy. Many only love winning elections and getting to do 100% of whatever they want. The real test is whether they love the institutions that sometimes make their side lose.

## Today's shortcut, tomorrow's environment

Economists know this argument well: time inconsistency, credibility, dynamic effects, the difference between today's policy and tomorrow's equilibrium. My mentors from Latin America understood it through the harshest possible experience. They had lived through capital flight, hyperinflation, military dictatorships, and the long, painful process of rebuilding credibility.

I never saw any of that. I am thirty-six. My political memory begins inside the Great Moderation, in a Colombia that had stabilized macroeconomically and was negotiating its way out of an armed conflict that was about something other than economic mismanagement. For a long time, to my generation, the older cautionary tales sounded like distant history (not so for my Venezuelan and Nicaraguan friends).

The lack of exposure to those crises seems to be denting the consensus my older colleagues helped to build. Populism is fashionable everywhere, and along with populism comes a loss of confidence in democratic institutions. As Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt have argued, twenty-first-century democracies do not usually die by coup. They die slowly, at the hands of elected leaders who weaken institutions one rule at a time. Tanks are out. Constitutional reforms, captured agencies, pliant courts, and well-funded loyalty campaigns are in. The deeper loss is the consensus that some institutions are off-limits for ordinary partisan politics — the courts, the central bank, the electoral authority, the press.

This matters especially when popular and charismatic leaders are in power.

When the leader we like is in power, constraints start to look like obstacles. If the reform is good, why let courts delay it? If the people voted for change, why should a central bank, a judge, or an opposition governor stand in the way? The temptation does not respect ideology or geography. 

But politics is not a one-shot game. Today's institutional shortcut becomes tomorrow's institutional environment.

If firms learn that investment depends on political loyalty, they stop competing on productivity and start competing on access. If judges learn that promotion depends on pleasing the ruling movement, legal reasoning becomes career management. If legislators learn that their future depends on obedience to the president, Congress becomes expensive decoration. If citizens learn that losing an election means losing protection, politics becomes existential.

Everyone learns where power lives. Everyone adapts. That is how a country changes equilibrium — not always with a coup, now more often with applause, public consultations, and the slow conversion of independent institutions into offices of the ruling party.

The stupidest moment to weaken constraints is when the president, or the ruling party, is popular.

## Mexico — the party that can't be stopped

As I mentioned in [my previous entry](#), I am relatively new to Mexico, and I have been reading about the history of the country I now live in. The current government has been systematically eroding checks and balances: closing watchdogs, weakening regulatory agencies, and passing a judicial reform that turns Supreme Court justices into elected officials, more like politicians than jurists. But this is not the first time a single political party has taken over Mexico's institutions. For Mexico this looks more like a regression to the mean than an exception: seventy-one years of PRI hegemony, and before that a bloody revolution, before that a long dictatorship, before that a series of very unstable governments, before that two major wars, and before that two failed monarchies. Sometimes it can seem as if Mexico simply does not like democracy.

The old PRI system was not a cartoon dictatorship. It allowed elections, opposition, courts, congress, unions, business groups, newspapers — controlled pluralism, managed opposition, corporatist bargaining. The point was never to abolish every institution. The point was to make sure every important institution understood where real power lived.

Morena — the current ruling party — may have done things many Mexicans like. That is not the issue. Every hegemonic party survives by giving enough people something they value. The PRI also built roads, distributed benefits, managed unions, and gave millions of people a place inside the system. Authoritarianism does not require a government to do nothing useful. It requires power to stop being contestable.

In Mexico today the discourse is redistributive in costume. The practice is much older: corporatism, nationalism, political loyalty, discretionary favors, crony capitalism. The state does not need to eliminate private interests. It can organize them around political loyalty — which firms get contracts, which judges get promotion, which governors get resources, which opponents get the lesson.

The tragedy is that many Mexicans think they are burying neoliberalism, when they may be resurrecting the PRI.

## The United States — exceptionalism shattered

When Antonin Scalia made his argument, he was clearly proud of American institutions. And the United States is different from Mexico in obvious ways. Its Constitution is older and harder to amend, its federalism is real, its courts enjoy enormous autonomy, and the opposition still controls universities, states, media organizations, law firms, cities, and large parts of civil society. The American state is not easy to capture.

That is precisely why the current deterioration is so interesting.

Since January 2025, a sitting American president has, taken together, done things that no longer leave much room for the word _hypothetical_. He launched military operations abroad — most prominently the June 2025 strikes on Iran — without seeking congressional authorization for war, despite the Constitution being quite clear on where that authority lies. He imposed sweeping tariffs through emergency powers; when the Supreme Court struck them down in early 2026, on the straightforward constitutional ground that tariff-setting power belongs to Congress, he immediately announced that he would reimpose them through alternative statutes, and Congress did nothing to reassert the authority the Court had just reminded everyone it possessed.[^us-events1]

He also attempted to remove Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook on disputed allegations of mortgage fraud — the first time a sitting president has tried to dismiss a Fed governor. At the same time, he pushed for, and obtained, the appointment of Kevin Warsh as Fed Chair. Warsh secured the nomination in part by aligning himself with the president's attacks on the central bank and his calls for looser monetary policy, despite Warsh's own earlier and very public arguments in favor of tighter monetary policy.[^us-events2]

The American constitutional architecture is still standing. The political habit of using it the way it was meant to be used is not.

The deeper problem is that partisanship has become stronger than institutional loyalty. This is not uniquely Republican, although the current Republican Party has made it impossible to ignore. It is a general temptation in presidential democracy. When the president is from my party, Congress should cooperate. When the courts block my policy, the courts are illegitimate. When the central bank resists my preferred macroeconomic fantasy, the central bank is anti-democratic. When the press investigates me, it is the enemy.

Every faction eventually discovers that constraints are useful when the other side governs and intolerable when its own side does. That is constitutional opportunism.

The US founders understood ambition better than virtue. They designed a system in which ambition would counteract ambition. The problem is that institutional incentives are mediated by parties. If a legislator cares more about defending the president of his party than defending the power of Congress, the branch does not check the branch. The party protects the leader.

At that point, separation of powers becomes separation of letterhead.

## Peru, again

Back to Peru, briefly, because Peru deserves more than the lunch story but less than the rest of the region.

The Peruvian constitution gives Congress and the president weapons against each other. In theory this should force negotiation and cooperation. In practice it has turned politics into permanent hostage-taking. Congress acts less like a legislature than like a demolition crew. Nobody has enough authority to govern, but everyone has enough power to destroy. Peru held general elections last April and is preparing for a runoff on June 7. I hope this time they manage to elect a more competent president than the recent rotation has produced.

A country can suffer because the executive has too much power, as in Mexico. It can also suffer because the executive has too little, as in Peru. In both cases, democracy decays because power is not organized into productive tension. It is monopolized in one case and weaponized in the other.

A branch should not be able to swallow the rest of the state. But a government also needs enough stability to fulfill its basic functions. The trick is keeping both.

## Colombia — more complicated than it looks

Back to my country.

Colombian presidents have historically enjoyed congressional support for their agendas, thanks to a long tradition of clientelism. The result, often praised under the label of *gobernabilidad*, was a kind of political stability bought through patronage — useful for governing, dangerous for democracy. Nobel laureate James Robinson, who has written extensively on institutions, has pointed to clientelism as one of the reasons the Colombian state has so often been unresponsive to the needs of the people it claims to serve.[^robinson-note] Congress in that arrangement was rarely a serious check on the executive; the courts did most of the checking, especially during Álvaro Uribe's two terms (2002–2010).

It is only with Petro that Congress itself has begun to behave like a coequal branch — and that both Congress and the courts have actively imposed limits on a sitting president. On the economic side, the 1991 constitution gave the central bank a strong measure of independence; the Banco de la República has since been crucial in maintaining macroeconomic stability, after decades of double-digit inflation before that reform. Despite all of its imperfections, Colombia has done something close to what a constitutional democracy is supposed to do: it has let elected presidents govern, but not govern alone.

Petro's reaction to being constrained has been damaging. Instead of treating institutional resistance as part of democracy, he treats it as sabotage. Congress blocks a reform, so the problem becomes Congress. Courts limit a decree, so the problem becomes the courts. The Constitution does its job, so the solution becomes rewriting the Constitution.

That is why the campaign for a constituent assembly matters. It is not only a legal proposal. It is a theory of power: if existing institutions do not approve the project, replace the institutional arrangement.

Iván Cepeda, the candidate of continuity inside Petro's political world, has tried to present a softer version: national agreement, dialogue, political reform. The ambiguity is doing a lot of work. *El País* put it well: Cepeda has only half-distanced himself from the constituent assembly Petro keeps pushing.[^colombia-elpais] Around him, the government is already pushing constitutional change. *La Silla Vacía* describes the constituent project as a way for Petro to keep doing politics beyond 2026 — shifting the center of gravity from Congress and the courts to a more direct appeal to "the people."[^colombia-silla] Cepeda can say *dialogue* while the president's office keeps building the machine. The irony, hard to miss, is that toward the end of his term Petro has been resorting to exactly the clientelist practices that secured congressional cooperation for his predecessors — using the system he said he could dismantle.

Colombia is therefore at a crossroads. Not because it has lost separation of powers, but because separation of powers has actually constrained the government, and the government's answer is to ask for a new game. That is the danger. Not that institutions failed. That they worked, and the president decided this was intolerable.

Analysts used to say Colombian democracy improved because Petro was allowed to win. There is truth in that. A democracy where the left cannot win is not a democracy. But a democracy where the left wins and tries to weaken the institutions that made alternation possible is also in serious trouble. The test was never whether Petro could arrive. The test is whether his movement is willing leave.
## The architecture that makes power fight power

Political systems are dynamic in both directions. Hungary is the recent reminder. Viktor Orbán spent years building a system designed to make alternation harder: media capture, institutional loyalists, legal advantages, nationalist polarization. And yet, in April 2026, he lost. The opposition won. Power became contestable again. Hungary is not fixed — institutions do not regenerate in a weekend — but the defeat matters because it shows that even after years of damage, politics is not always closed.

The hopeful lesson is that power can still be made contestable.

A democracy is not a system where my side wins. It is one where my side can lose and still live to compete again — where losing an election does not mean losing legal protection, access to courts, property, speech, professional survival, or the possibility of future victory.

This is why people need to love separation of powers. Not admire it vaguely. Love it in the specific moments when it frustrates them. Love the court that blocks a policy you support because the executive exceeded its authority. Love the gridlock, even when your side could otherwise be getting things done. Especially then.

Have strong convictions. Argue hard. Win elections. Pass reforms. Defeat bad ideas. But do not confuse victory with ownership of the state. The state is not a prize to be captured by the moral side of history. It is a dangerous machine that must be divided, because everyone who controls it eventually finds reasons to use it for more than they promised.

That is Scalia's uncomfortable point. The real protection is not the beautiful list of rights. It is the architecture that makes power fight power.

I think about that lunch sometimes. My Peruvian colleagues were not telling me a horror story. They were describing how a country can have a constitution, a Congress, a president, courts, elections, and an opposition, and still wake up unable to govern itself. They were not arguing for an all-powerful executive. They were arguing for a system where checks and balances actually balance, instead of just blocking each other.

Rights on paper are easy.

The hard part is keeping someone strong enough to say no, and honest about when to say it.

In ten days, my country votes.
