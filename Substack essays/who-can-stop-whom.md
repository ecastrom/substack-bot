# Who can stop whom?

**Why the stupidest moment to weaken separation of powers is when you like the president.**

Several weeks ago I had lunch with a couple of Peruvian colleagues. The conversation drifted, as it does among economists, from data to gossip to whatever weirdness one of us had run into that week. At some point we landed on Peru. Peru comes up often in my work and in my conversations. My boss is Peruvian. So is my PhD advisor, who is also a close friend. So is my research assistant. At some point, Peru stops being a case study and becomes part of the furniture of my intellectual life.

Peru has had eight presidents in the last ten years, a real oddity even by Latin American standards — and Latin America is the region that has tried almost every form of government on offer. The most recent removal happened in February 2026. One of my colleagues offered a hypothesis about that impeachment that stayed with me. Congress did not really want to remove the president, he argued. Not fully. The impeachment vote was performative, a political signal before the next election, a way for legislators to show loyalty to their platform. The vote was theater. Then the theater became reality. An ally was named interim president, partly to avoid stirring the mud even more. The country woke up with a new government because its politicians had grown accustomed to playing with loaded institutional weapons.

I have been thinking about that lunch ever since. Not because Peru is the worst case in Latin America — it is not — but because Peru punishes the simple version of every argument about democracy. The simple version goes: presidents bad, congress good; or executives bad, courts good; or, more generally, separation of powers is just constraints, and constraints are just safeguards. Peru is what happens when the constraints exist and the politicians have stopped paying any cost for using them. It is the case that made me see something I had stopped seeing — that the most important question in a democracy is not *who wins?* but *who can stop whom?*

## The loser's power

Antonin Scalia [once made a point](https://x.com/i/status/2025152903921475665) that has aged disturbingly well. Americans, he said, often think their freedoms come from the Bill of Rights. But many authoritarian constitutions also contain beautiful promises. The Soviet Constitution had rights on paper. So do many regimes nobody would confuse with free societies. The difference is not the poetry of the legal text. The difference is the structure of power.

The most important democratic institution is not the election. It is the loser's ability to still have power.

Congress must still matter when the president is popular. Courts must still matter when the majority is angry. Central banks must still matter when politicians want cheap credit. Local governments must still matter when the national government wants uniform obedience. The opposition must still matter after losing.

That is what separation of powers is for. And in the same conversation about rights on paper, Scalia said something else that I keep returning to: *we need to learn to love the gridlock.* The branches were never supposed to agree easily. The whole point of the architecture is that they slow each other down, that no one of them can simply move when it wants to. The frustration is the point.

People say they love democracy. Many only love winning elections. The real test is whether they love the institutions that sometimes make their side lose.

## Today's shortcut, tomorrow's environment

Economists know the argument well: time inconsistency, credibility, dynamic effects, the difference between today's policy and tomorrow's equilibrium. The older Latin American economists I trained with knew it from the worst possible version of it. They had lived through capital flight, hyperinflation, military dictatorships, and the slow rebuilding of credibility that someone in their family had already paid for.

I never saw any of that. I am thirty-six. My political memory begins inside the Great Moderation, in a Colombia that had stabilized macroeconomically and was negotiating its way out of an armed conflict that was about something other than economic mismanagement. For a long time, to my generation, the older cautionary tales sounded like history.

That was the generational mistake. As Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt have argued, twenty-first-century democracies do not usually die by coup. They die slowly, at the hands of elected leaders who weaken institutions one rule at a time. Tanks are out. Constitutional reforms, captured agencies, pliant courts, and well-funded loyalty campaigns are in. What is being lost is the consensus that some institutions are off-limits for ordinary partisan politics — the courts, the central bank, the electoral authority, the press. That consensus, more than the absence of tanks, was the difference between the bad old days and the calmer ones.

The trouble is that knowing better is not the same as doing better. When the leader we like is in power, constraints start to look like obstacles. If the reform is good, why let courts delay it? If the people voted for change, why should a central bank, a judge, or an opposition governor stand in the way? The temptation does not respect ideology or geography. Latin American economists, who lean right by local standards, feel it when a market reformer arrives. Heterodox economists feel it when a redistributive coalition takes office. American economists, who lean Democratic these days, feel it when their team wins. Everyone, eventually, finds a leader good enough that the rules look optional.

But politics is not a one-shot game. Today's institutional shortcut becomes tomorrow's institutional environment.

If firms learn that investment depends on political loyalty, they stop competing on productivity and start competing on access. If judges learn that promotion depends on pleasing the ruling movement, legal reasoning becomes career management. If legislators learn that their future depends on obedience to the president, Congress becomes expensive decoration. If citizens learn that losing an election means losing protection, politics becomes existential.

Everyone learns where power lives. Everyone adapts. That is how a country changes equilibrium — not always with a coup, often with applause, public consultations, and the slow conversion of independent institutions into offices of the ruling movement.

The stupidest moment to weaken constraints is when you like the president.

## Mexico — when no one can be stopped

I have been reading about the history of the country I am now living in. As I mentioned in [my previous entry](#), I am relatively new to Mexico. The country is becoming yet another case in the region — though, looking at the longer arc, *becoming* is the wrong verb. The most recent government has been systematically eroding checks and balances, closing watchdogs, weakening independent regulatory agencies, and — crucially — pushing through a judicial reform that turns Supreme Court justices into elected officials, more like politicians than jurists. But this is not the first time a political party has taken over the country's democratic institutions. For Mexico this looks more like a regression to the mean than an exception: seventy-one years of PRI hegemony, and before that a bloody revolution, before that a long dictatorship, before that a series of very unstable governments, before that two major wars, and before that two failed monarchies. Sometimes it can seem as if Mexico simply does not like democracy.

The old PRI system was not a cartoon dictatorship. It allowed elections, opposition, courts, congress, unions, business groups, newspapers — controlled pluralism, managed opposition, corporatist bargaining, nationalist rhetoric, selective redistribution. The point was never to abolish every institution. The point was to make sure every important institution understood where real power lived.

Morena may have done things many Mexicans like. That is not the issue. Every hegemonic party survives by giving enough people something they value. The PRI also built roads, distributed benefits, managed unions, and gave millions of people a place inside the system. Authoritarianism does not require a government to do nothing useful. It requires power to stop being contestable.

The discourse is redistributive in costume. The practice is much older: corporatism, nationalism, political loyalty, discretionary favors, crony capitalism. The state does not need to eliminate private interests. It can organize them around political loyalty — which firms get contracts, which unions get protection, which media outlets get advertising, which judges get promotion, which governors get resources, which opponents get the lesson. This is why judicial independence matters so much. A judge elected through a political machine is not obviously more democratic than an independent one. She is just more dependent on whoever can mobilize votes.

The tragedy is that many Mexicans think they are burying neoliberalism, when they may be resurrecting the PRI.

## American exceptionalism, shattered

When Scalia made that argument he was clearly proud of American institutions, and there is no question that the United States is different from Mexico in obvious ways. Its constitutional structure is older, harder to amend. Its federalism is real. Its courts still have enormous autonomy. Its opposition still controls states, universities, media outlets, law firms, cities, and civil society. The American state is not easy to capture.

But that is exactly why the current deterioration is interesting.

The deterioration is no longer a hypothesis. Since January 2025, a sitting president has pardoned a wide swath of those convicted in connection with the January 6 attack on the Capitol; imposed sweeping tariffs by emergency-powers decree while a compliant Congress declined to reclaim the authority it formally retains; openly pressured the Federal Reserve to cut interest rates and floated the removal of its chair; directed the Department of Justice toward investigations of his political opponents; and attacked individual federal judges by name. The constitutional architecture is still standing. The political habit of using it the way it was meant to be used is not.

The deeper problem is that partisanship has become stronger than institutional loyalty. This is not a uniquely Republican disease, although the current Republican Party has made it impossible to ignore. It is a general temptation in presidential democracy. When the president is from my party, Congress should cooperate. When the courts block my policy, the courts are illegitimate. When the central bank resists my preferred macroeconomic fantasy, the central bank is anti-democratic. When the bureaucracy slows me down, it is the deep state. When the press investigates me, it is the enemy.

Every faction eventually discovers that constraints are useful when the other side governs and intolerable when its own side does. That is not constitutionalism. That is constitutional opportunism.

The American founders understood ambition better than virtue. They designed a system in which ambition would counteract ambition. It is a hard public-choice claim, often treated like a civics-class ornament: do not rely on moral restraint when you can create institutional incentives. The problem is that institutional incentives are mediated by parties. If a legislator cares more about defending the president of his party than about defending the power of Congress, the branch does not check the branch. The party protects the leader.

At that point, separation of powers becomes separation of letterhead.

## Peru, again — the case for dysfunction

Back to Peru, which deserves more than the lunch story.

The Peruvian constitution gives Congress and the president weapons against each other. In theory this should force negotiation. In practice it has turned politics into permanent hostage-taking. Presidents govern with one eye on impeachment. Congress acts less like a legislature than like a demolition crew. Nobody has enough authority to govern, but everyone has enough power to destroy.

The easy reading of this is dysfunction. There is a less obvious reading worth taking seriously, though, and it has been nagging at me since that lunch. The recent occupants of the Peruvian presidency have been, with painful consistency, candidates few Peruvians would actually defend. The political culture that selects mediocre presidents is also selecting frivolous legislators — but at least it is selecting them in tension with one another. A constitutional system that reliably stops bad presidents from getting much done is doing some work. Whether it is doing enough is another question.

So before judging Peru too harshly, it is worth pausing. The country pays real costs for all that institutional combat — frequent crises, weak governance, episodes that look like collapse from outside. But the deeper machinery may be more dynamically efficient than it appears. It is at least refusing to let any one bad actor consolidate. The result is not liberty, exactly. It is dysfunction. But perhaps a necessary one. *Love the gridlock*, Scalia said. Sometimes dysfunction is not a bug but a feature of the system.

Peru held general elections on April 12 and 13, 2026, with the familiar promise that the next configuration might finally be more stable. I hope so. The deeper problem will not be solved by changing the names on the ballot, because the problem is not only the names. It is the political culture from which both presidents and legislators are drawn — and no constitutional design, gridlock or otherwise, can fix that on its own.

Mexico is the opposite case. A country can suffer because the executive has too much power, as in Mexico. It can also suffer because the executive has too little stability, as in Peru. In both cases democracy decays because power is not organized into productive tension. It is monopolized in one case and weaponized in the other. The difference is that Peru, for all its theatrics, still has the architecture of contestation. Mexico is in the process of losing it.

A president should not be able to swallow the rest of the state. But a president also needs enough stability to govern. The trick is keeping both.

## Colombia — more complicated than it looks

Colombia is the more interesting case because, so far, the checks and balances have actually worked. But the story is more complicated than that headline suggests, and an honest version requires acknowledging how those institutions performed before Petro.

Colombian presidents have historically enjoyed congressional support for their agendas, thanks to a long-held tradition of clientelism. The result, often praised under the label of *gobernabilidad*, was a kind of political stability bought through patronage — useful for governing, dangerous for democracy. Nobel laureate James Robinson, who has written extensively on Colombian institutions, has pointed to clientelism as one reason the Colombian state has so often been unresponsive to the needs of the people it claims to serve.[^robinson-note] Congress in that arrangement was rarely a serious check on the executive; the courts did most of that work, especially during Uribe's two terms. It is only with Petro that Congress itself has begun to behave like a coequal branch — and that both Congress and the courts have actively imposed limits on a sitting president. The irony is that toward the end of his term, Petro has been resorting to the same clientelist practices that secured congressional cooperation for his predecessors.

On the economic-policy side, the 1991 constitution gave the central bank a strong measure of independence. Inflation came under control after having been perennially in the double digits. The Banco de la República, as an independent institution, has been crucial in maintaining macroeconomic stability and a low, stable inflation rate. Despite its imperfections, Colombia has done something close to what a constitutional democracy is supposed to do: it has let elected presidents govern, but not govern alone.

This is the case that made me stop postponing the essay.

Petro's reaction has been revealing. Instead of treating institutional resistance as part of democracy, he often treats it as sabotage. Congress blocks a reform, so the problem becomes Congress. Courts limit a decree, so the problem becomes the courts. The Constitution does its job, so the solution becomes rewriting the Constitution. That is why the campaign for a constituent assembly matters. It is not only a legal proposal. It is a theory of power: if existing institutions do not approve the project, replace the institutional arrangement.

Iván Cepeda, the candidate of continuity inside Petro's political world, has tried to present a softer version: national agreement, dialogue, political reform. The ambiguity is doing a lot of work. *El País* put it well: Cepeda has only half-distanced himself from the constituent assembly Petro keeps pushing. The final agreement with the Greens (Alianza Verde) mentioned the idea only in passing, without Cepeda explicitly closing the door to it, while volunteers collected signatures for the constituent assembly at his campaign events.[^colombia-elpais]

Meanwhile the current government is already pushing constitutional change. *La Silla Vacía* has described Petro's constituent project as a way to keep doing politics beyond 2026, stressing its political function: mobilization, pressure, and the attempt to shift the center of gravity from Congress and the courts to a more direct appeal to "the people."[^colombia-silla] The successor can say "dialogue" while the president's office builds the machine.

Colombia is therefore at a crossroads — not because it has already lost separation of powers, but because separation of powers has actually constrained the government, and the government's answer is to ask for a new game. That is the danger. Not that institutions failed. That they worked, and the president decided this was intolerable.

Analysts used to say Colombian democracy improved because Petro was allowed to win. There is truth in that. A democracy where the left cannot win is not a democracy. But a democracy where the left wins and tries to weaken the institutions that made alternation possible is also in trouble. The test is not whether Petro could arrive. The test is whether his movement can leave without rewriting the rules on the way out.

## Hope, but not cheap hope

There is some hope. Not the cheap kind. History is not nice. Latin America has experimented with almost every form of government: military dictatorship, revolutionary socialism, oligarchic republics, populist presidencies, technocratic democracy, party hegemony, weak-state pluralism, constitutional assemblies, dollarized stability, inflationary chaos, guerrilla war, negotiated peace. The region should know better, but knowing better is not the same as doing better.

Still, political systems are dynamic in both directions.

Hungary is a useful reminder. Viktor Orbán spent years building a system designed to make alternation harder: media capture, institutional loyalists, legal advantages, nationalist polarization. And yet, in April 2026, he lost. The opposition won. Power became contestable again. That does not mean Hungary is fixed. Entrenched loyalists remain. Institutions do not regenerate in a weekend. A hegemonic system leaves residue in courts, agencies, media, business networks, and habits of fear. But the defeat matters because it shows that even after years of damage, politics is not always closed.

The hopeful lesson is not that authoritarian projects always lose. They do not. The hopeful lesson is that power can still be made contestable if enough people defend the institutions that make losing possible.

Losing possible. That is the key phrase.

A democracy is not a system where my side wins. It is one where my side can lose and still live to compete again. Where losing an election does not mean losing legal protection, access to courts, property, speech, professional survival, or the possibility of future victory.

## The architecture that makes power fight power

This is why people need to love separation of powers. Not admire it vaguely. Love it in the specific moments when it frustrates them. Love the court that blocks a policy you support because the executive exceeded its authority. Love the boring procedural rule that prevents a temporary majority from becoming permanent power. Love the gridlock, even when your side could otherwise be getting things done. Especially then.

This is not moderation as personality. Have strong convictions. Argue hard. Win elections. Pass reforms. Build parties. Defeat bad ideas. But do not confuse victory with ownership of the state. The state is not a prize to be captured by the moral side of history. It is a dangerous machine that must be divided because everyone who controls it eventually finds reasons to use it for more than they promised.

That is Scalia's uncomfortable point. The real protection is not the beautiful list of rights. It is the architecture that makes power fight power.

I think about that lunch sometimes. My Peruvian colleagues were not telling me a horror story. They were describing, with the particular fatalism of people who have watched their own institutions misfire repeatedly, how a country can have a constitution, a Congress, a president, courts, elections, and an opposition, and still wake up some mornings unable to govern itself. They were not arguing for an all-powerful executive. They were arguing for a system where checks and balances actually balance, instead of just blocking each other.

Rights on paper are easy.

The hard part is keeping someone strong enough to say no, and honest about when to say it.
