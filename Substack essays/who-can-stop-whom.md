# The Loser's Power

**Why the stupidest moment to weaken constraints is when you like the president.**

Several weeks ago I had lunch with a couple of Peruvian colleagues. The conversation drifted, as it does among economists who have lived in too many countries, from data to gossip to whatever weirdness one of us had run into that week. At some point we landed on Peru. Peru comes up often in my work and conversations. My closest colleague, who is also my boss, is Peruvian. So is my PhD advisor, Cesar Martinelli. So is my research assistant. At some point, Peru stops being a case study and becomes part of the furniture of my intellectual life.

Peru has had eight presidents in the last ten years, a real oddity even by Latin American standards. The most recent removal happened in February 2026. One of my colleagues offered a hypothesis about that impeachment that stayed with me. Congress did not really want to remove the president, he argued. Not fully. The impeachment vote was performative, a political signal before the next election, a way for legislators to show loyalty to their platform. The vote was theater. Then the theater became reality. An ally was named interim president, partly to avoid stirring the mud even more. The country woke up with a new government because politicians had been playing with loaded institutional weapons.

I have been thinking about that lunch ever since. Not because Peru is the worst case in Latin America - it is not - but because Peru is the case that punishes the simple version of every argument about democracy. The simple version goes: presidents bad, congress good; or executives bad, courts good; or, more generally, separation of powers is just constraints, and constraints are just safeguards. Peru is what happens when constraints exist but everyone is willing to pull every lever at every moment.

I wanted to write the essay then. Instead, I went back to a field experiment I am working on, because papers have a way of disciplining your moral urgency. What brought the idea back was Colombia: an incoming presidential election, an incumbent president, Gustavo Petro, who is enjoying a spree of popularity after increasing the minimum wage in 24% and expanding the expenditure by taking expensive debt, is openly promoting a a new Constitution via an assembly (Asamblea Constituyente), and a chosen successor, Ivan Cepeda, who may speak more softly but has not clearly broken with the project.

In Peru, no one can govern. In most of the region, no one can be stopped.

Antonin Scalia once made a point that has aged disturbingly well. Americans, he said, often think their freedoms come from the Bill of Rights. But many authoritarian constitutions also contain beautiful promises. The Soviet Constitution had rights on paper. So do many regimes nobody would confuse with free societies. The difference is not the poetry of the legal text. The difference is the structure of power.

Who can stop whom?

That is the question behind most of the democratic deterioration we are seeing today. The global indexes - Freedom House, V-Dem, the Economist Intelligence Unit - all tell some version of the same story: democracy is declining, institutions are weakening, norms are eroding. These reports are useful, but they make the problem sound like weather. What it usually means in practice is something more specific: the institutions designed to divide power are being captured, intimidated, ignored, or turned into theater.

The most important democratic institution is not the election. It is the loser's ability to still have power.

Congress must still matter when the president is popular. Courts must still matter when the majority is angry. Central banks must still matter when politicians want cheap credit. Local governments must still matter when the national government wants uniform obedience. The opposition must still matter after losing.

People say they love democracy. Many only love winning elections. The real test is whether they love the institutions that sometimes make their side lose. And this will be especially important for my fellow Colombians who like the president.

## What economists should already know

This is where economists should be especially uncomfortable.

We spend our careers talking about incentives, credibility, time inconsistency, dynamic effects, institutional constraints. Then, in politics, many of us suddenly turn romantic. If the right leader wins, constraints are a nuisance. If the reform is good, why let courts delay it? If the people voted for change, why should a central bank, a judge, or an opposition governor stand in the way?

But politics is not a one-shot game. Today's institutional shortcut becomes tomorrow's institutional environment.

If firms learn that investment depends on political loyalty, they stop competing on productivity and start competing on access. If judges learn that promotion depends on pleasing the ruling movement, legal reasoning becomes career management. If legislators learn that their future depends on obedience to the president, Congress becomes expensive decoration. If citizens learn that losing an election means losing protection, politics becomes existential.

Everyone learns where power lives. Everyone adapts. That is how a country changes equilibrium - not always with a coup, often with applause, public consultations, and the slow conversion of independent institutions into offices of the ruling movement.

The stupidest moment to weaken constraints is when you like the president. That is also when people most want to do it. Separation of powers is not built for moments when everyone is calm and the president is modest. It is built for the moment when a popular leader says: the people are with me, therefore the institutions should move out of the way.

## Mexico is returning to something old

Mexico is the clearest case in the region. The country is not inventing something new. It is returning to something old with better branding.

For most of the twentieth century Mexico lived under a system where elections existed, opposition existed, courts existed, congress existed, unions existed, business groups existed, newspapers existed. The point was not to abolish every institution. The point was to make sure every important institution understood where real power lived.

That was the old PRI system. It was not a cartoon dictatorship. It allowed controlled pluralism, managed opposition, corporatist bargaining, nationalist rhetoric, selective redistribution, loyal unions, loyal business friends, and elections where the ruling party could lose some battles but not the system.

Morena may have done things many Mexicans like. That is not the issue. Every hegemonic party survives by giving enough people something they value. The PRI also built roads, distributed benefits, managed unions, and gave millions of people a place inside the system. Authoritarianism does not require a government to do nothing useful. It requires power to stop being contestable.

The discourse is redistributive in costume. The practice is much older: corporatism, nationalism, political loyalty, discretionary favors, and crony capitalism. The state does not need to eliminate private interests. It can organize them around political loyalty: deciding which firms receive contracts, which unions receive protection, which media outlets receive advertising, which judges receive promotion, which governors receive resources, and which opponents receive the lesson.

This is why judicial independence matters so much. A judge elected through a political machine is not obviously more democratic than an independent judge. She may simply be more dependent on whichever forces are capable of mobilizing votes. The rhetoric is that the people will control the judiciary. The risk is that the ruling party will.

The tragedy is that many Mexicans think they are burying neoliberalism, when they may be resurrecting the PRI.

## The United States is not exempt

The United States is different from Mexico in obvious ways. Its constitutional structure is older, harder to amend. Its federalism is real. Its courts still have enormous autonomy. Its opposition still controls states, universities, media outlets, law firms, cities, and civil society. The American state is not easy to capture.

But that is exactly why the deterioration is interesting.

The United States shows that formal separation of powers is not enough if political actors stop wanting to use it. Congress can have constitutional authority to check the president and still behave like a fan club. Courts can have independence and still face threats meant to make judges calculate the political cost of doing their job. The central bank can have formal autonomy and still become the target of presidential pressure. War powers can belong to Congress in theory and migrate to the executive in practice.

Checks and balances do not die only when dictators abolish them. They also die when voters treat them as annoying paperwork.

Trump is not the whole story. The deeper problem is that partisanship has become stronger than institutional loyalty. This is not only a Republican disease, although the current Republican Party has made it impossible to ignore. It is a general temptation in presidential democracy. When the president is from my party, Congress should cooperate. When the courts block my policy, the courts are illegitimate. When the central bank resists my preferred macroeconomic fantasy, the central bank is anti-democratic. When the bureaucracy slows me down, the bureaucracy is the deep state. When the press investigates me, the press is the enemy.

Every faction eventually discovers that constraints are useful when the other side governs and intolerable when its own side does.

That is not constitutionalism. That is constitutional opportunism.

The American founders understood ambition better than virtue. They did not design a system that required politicians to be angels. They designed one in which ambition would counteract ambition. The famous line is sometimes treated like a civics-class ornament, but it is a hard public-choice claim: do not rely on moral restraint when you can create institutional incentives. The problem is that institutional incentives are mediated by parties. If a legislator cares more about defending the president of his party than defending the power of Congress, the branch does not check the branch. The party protects the leader.

At that point, separation of powers becomes separation of letterhead.

## Peru, again

Back to Peru, which deserves more than the lunch story.

The Peruvian constitution gives Congress and the president weapons against each other. In theory this should force negotiation. In practice it has turned politics into permanent hostage-taking. Presidents govern with one eye on impeachment. Congress acts less like a legislature than like a demolition crew. Nobody has enough authority to govern, but everyone has enough power to destroy.

The result is not liberty. It is dysfunction.

Peru held general elections on April 12 and 13, 2026, with the familiar promise that the next configuration might finally be more stable. I hope so. But the deeper problem is not solved by changing the names on the ballot.

A country can suffer because the executive has too much power, as in Mexico. It can also suffer because the executive has too little stability, as in Peru. In both cases democracy decays because power is not organized into productive tension. It is monopolized in one case and weaponized in the other.

This is where Scalia's preference for American-style separation becomes useful even for those of us who do not share his politics. He criticized parliamentary systems because governments can fall too easily. Peru is not parliamentary, but it has acquired one of the worst parliamentary habits: government can collapse as political theater. It has the instability without the discipline, the crisis without the responsibility.

A president should not be able to swallow the rest of the state. But a president also needs enough stability to govern. Otherwise democracy becomes theater performed by people holding knives under the table.

## Colombia, where the system is still fighting back

Colombia is the more interesting case because, so far, the checks and balances have worked. Congress has blocked parts of Petro's agenda. Courts have constrained executive decrees. The central bank has not become an office of the presidency. Reelection is not available. In institutional terms, Colombia has done what a constitutional democracy is supposed to do: it has let an elected president govern, but not govern alone.

This is the case that made me stop postponing the essay.

Petro's reaction has been revealing. Instead of treating institutional resistance as part of democracy, he often treats it as sabotage. Congress blocks a reform, so the problem becomes Congress. Courts limit a decree, so the problem becomes the courts. The Constitution does its job, so the solution becomes rewriting the Constitution.

That is why the campaign for a constituent assembly matters. It is not only a legal proposal. It is a theory of power: if existing institutions do not approve the project, replace the institutional arrangement.

Ivan Cepeda, the candidate of continuity inside Petro's political world, has tried to present a softer version: national agreement, dialogue, political reform. The ambiguity is doing a lot of work. El Pais described it well: Cepeda has tried to distance himself only halfway from the constituent assembly that Petro keeps agitating. The final agreement with the Greens mentioned the idea only lightly, without Cepeda explicitly closing the door to it, while people collected signatures for the constituent assembly around his campaign event.[^colombia-elpais]

Around him, the current government is already pushing constitutional change. La Silla Vacia has described Petro's constituent project as a way to keep doing politics beyond 2026, and has emphasized the political function of the proposal: mobilization, pressure, and the attempt to move the center of gravity from Congress and the courts to a more direct appeal to "the people."[^colombia-silla] The successor can say "dialogue" while the president's office builds the machine.

Colombia is therefore at a crossroads - not because it has already lost separation of powers, but because separation of powers has actually constrained the government, and the government's answer is to ask for a new game.

That is the danger. Not that institutions failed. That they worked, and the president decided this was intolerable.

Analysts used to say Colombian democracy improved because Petro was allowed to win. There is truth in that. A democracy where the left cannot win is not a democracy. But a democracy where the left wins and tries to weaken the institutions that made alternation possible is also in trouble. The test is not whether Petro could arrive. The test is whether his movement can leave without rewriting the rules on the way out.

## Hope, but not cheap hope

There is some hope. Not the cheap kind. History is not nice. Latin America has experimented with almost every form of government: military dictatorship, revolutionary socialism, oligarchic republics, populist presidencies, technocratic democracy, party hegemony, weak-state pluralism, constitutional assemblies, dollarized stability, inflationary chaos, guerrilla war, negotiated peace. The region should know better, but knowing better is not the same as doing better.

Still, political systems are dynamic in both directions.

Hungary is a useful reminder. Viktor Orban spent years building a system designed to make alternation harder: media capture, institutional loyalists, legal advantages, nationalist polarization. And yet, in April 2026, he lost. The opposition won. Power became contestable again.

That does not mean Hungary is fixed. Entrenched loyalists remain. Institutions do not regenerate in a weekend. A hegemonic system leaves residue in courts, agencies, media, business networks, and habits of fear. But the defeat matters because it shows that even after years of damage, politics is not always closed.

The hopeful lesson is not that authoritarian projects always lose. They do not. The hopeful lesson is that power can still be made contestable if enough people defend the institutions that make losing possible.

Losing possible. That is the key phrase.

A democracy is not a system where my side wins. It is one where my side can lose and still live to compete again. Where losing an election does not mean losing legal protection, access to courts, property, speech, professional survival, or the possibility of future victory.

This is why people need to love separation of powers. Not admire it vaguely. Love it in the specific moments when it frustrates them. Love the court that blocks a policy you support because the executive exceeded its authority. Love the boring procedural rule that prevents a temporary majority from becoming permanent power.

This is not moderation as personality. Have strong convictions. Argue hard. Win elections. Pass reforms. Build parties. Defeat bad ideas. But do not confuse victory with ownership of the state. The state is not a prize to be captured by the moral side of history. It is a dangerous machine that must be divided because everyone who controls it eventually discovers reasons to use it for more than they promised.

That is Scalia's uncomfortable point. The real protection is not the beautiful list of rights. It is the architecture that makes power fight power.

I think about that lunch sometimes. My Peruvian colleagues were not telling me a horror story. They were describing, with the particular fatalism of people who have watched their own institutions misfire repeatedly, how a country can have a constitution, a Congress, a president, courts, elections, and an opposition, and still wake up some mornings unable to govern itself. They were not arguing for an all-powerful executive. They were arguing for a system where checks and balances actually balance, instead of just blocking each other.

Rights on paper are easy.

The hard part is keeping someone strong enough to say no, and honest about when to say it.
