# Style guide for the Substack essays

Derived from the three published pieces on edgarcastrom.substack.com (read 2026-10-29) and the drafts in this folder. Where a rule has a number attached, the number was measured on the published text, not invented. Use this guide when drafting or reviewing; the older bot-era rules in `edgar_profile.md` and `content_generator.py` were written for 500-character Notes and are superseded where they conflict.

Reference texts, in order of weight:

1. "¿Quién puede detener a quién?" (May 2026, Spanish, ~2,800 words). The model for long Spanish essays.
2. "My Favorite Coworker Complains a Lot" (April 2026, English, ~1,750 words). The model for English essays and for essay shape in general.
3. "Empezar antes no es lo mismo que empezar bien" (October 2026, ~300 words, LinkedIn repost). A note, not an essay. Its rules differ and are marked below.

---

## 1. What an essay is here

An essay is one load-bearing idea, carried by a personal anchor, tested against two to four real cases, and closed by returning to the anchor. Everything else is support.

The shape that both long essays share:

1. **A scene the author was actually in** (a lunch, a coworker, a conversation). Two to four paragraphs. It is specific: who was there, what they said, why it stuck.
2. **The question or thesis, stated plainly, at the end of the scene.** *"The most important question in a democracy is not who wins? but who can stop whom?"* / *"Complaining, properly understood, is one of the most underrated social technologies we have."*
3. **The economist's frame**, introduced through a named person and a named work: Hirschman and *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty*; Scalia on rights on paper; Levitsky and Ziblatt; Olson. One frame, not three.
4. **Cases.** Real places, real institutions, real numbers: Boeing, Tenerife, the Toyota andon cord; Peru's eight presidents, Mexico's judicial reform, the Banco de la República. Each case does one job for the argument.
5. **The honest complication.** A section that concedes what the other side gets right, or where the author's own case cuts against him. *"A short cultural aside, handled carefully"*; *"Colombia: más complicado de lo que parece"*; the Peru footnote conceding that removing incompetent presidents is also what institutions are for.
6. **Return to the anchor, then one short closing line.** *"I am glad I get to work with one."* / *"En diez días, mi país vota."*

Length: 1,700 to 2,900 words. The reference English essay is 1,750; the Spanish one 2,800 because it carries four country cases. Length is earned by cases, not by restatement.

Section headers: 4 to 6. Headers are phrases that advance the argument, not labels. *"What silence costs"*, *"El atajo de hoy, la constitución de mañana"*, *"Complaint as a public good"*. Never repeat the title as a header. Never a header called "Conclusion".

---

## 2. Title and subtitle

**Title:** short, concrete, often a question or a line someone could say out loud. *My Favorite Coworker Complains a Lot.* *¿Quién puede detener a quién?* Four to seven words.

**Subtitle:** one bold sentence that states the thesis, not the topic. It should be possible to disagree with it.

- Yes: *Why the person who names the problem out loud is doing unpaid public work — and why teams without one are quietly in trouble.*
- Yes: *Por qué el momento más estúpido para una constituyente es cuando hay un presidente con amplio apoyo popular.*
- No: *Sobre las cosas que más valoramos y las oportunidades que dejamos pasar.* (This describes; it does not claim.)

---

## 3. Opening

Open inside a scene, in the first person, within the first sentence. The reader should know by the end of paragraph one that a specific person is talking about a specific experience.

- *"My favorite coworker complains a lot."*
- *"Hace varias semanas almorcé con un par de colegas peruanos."*

Do not open with a generalization about the age we live in, the state of society, or what "we" want. *"Nuestra idea de lo que significa vivir bien ha cambiado profundamente"* is the opening to avoid: true, unfalsifiable, and anyone could have written it.

Do not open with "As an economist" or any variant. The economics arrives through what the author notices, not through credentials. Mentioning that he teaches economics mid-essay, as a fact about his life, is fine.

The short note may open with a hypothetical (*"Imaginemos a dos estudiantes"*). The essay does not.

---

## 4. Argument

**One spine.** Every section should be removable only at visible cost to the thesis. If a section could be cut and the ending would still follow, cut it or fold it in.

**The signature move is the dynamic-incentives reframe.** The essays are at their best when they take a static moral judgment ("this is dysfunctional", "this is repugnant", "this person is annoying") and ask what equilibrium it produces over time, who learns what, and who pays. *"Today's institutional shortcut becomes tomorrow's institutional environment."* *"The person who complains pays the cost; everyone else collects the benefit."* *"Quienes más sienten esas consecuencias no siempre son quienes defienden las prohibiciones."* Build toward that move; do not bury it.

**Who pays is always on the table.** The classical-liberal lens in these essays is not "markets good"; it is "name the cost of the rule, and name who bears it". Keep that framing even when the conclusion favors the restriction.

**Concede honestly, once, in full.** Each essay gives the opposing view its best paragraph: AMLO's critique of autonomous agencies "no era enteramente injusta"; the organizational-silence literature's distinction between constructive and chronic complaint; the exploitation risks in surrogacy. Do it once, completely, then move on. Repeating the concession every few paragraphs reads as nervousness, not fairness.

**Do not end on a research agenda.** "We need more evidence" can be one sentence inside the essay. It cannot be the last section. The essays end on a conviction and a scene, with the uncertainty acknowledged earlier.

---

## 5. Evidence

- **Real cases over hypotheticals.** In the long essays every example is a named place, person, institution, or study. A hypothetical couple or imagined firm is for the 300-word note, not the essay.
- **Named scholars, named works, specific findings.** Hirschman (1970), Edmondson's nursing units, Olson, Becker and Elías's 12 % estimate, Levitsky and Ziblatt. One sentence of who they are if the general reader would not know.
- **Numbers that change the argument, and only those.** Eight presidents in ten years; 1,8 hijos por mujer; two million near-miss reports since 1976. A number used as decoration is cut.
- **Verify every number twice, against the primary source, before publishing.** Write down the source next to the number in the draft. The review of the surrogacy draft found two numbers that did not match their sources; that is the failure mode this rule exists to prevent.
- **State the scope of an estimate.** "Kidneys, in the United States, a theoretical estimate" rather than "organs". "A 14-country survey" rather than "an international survey".
- **Mark uncertainty at the level of the claim, not the sentence.** One clear "this is a theoretical estimate, not an observed outcome" beats hedging every verb. See §7 on hedge density.
- **Footnotes carry the nuance, the body carries the argument.** The Peru footnote in the Spanish essay is the model: the body makes the strong claim, the footnote says "hay que matizar" and gives the counter-case.

## 6. Voice

**Register:** a sharp economist thinking out loud at the lunch table with colleagues he respects. Casual syntax, rigorous content. Wry, not jokey. The farmer joke ("Soy un pésimo agricultor") is the right dose of humor: one line, self-directed, in service of the point.

**First person, with stakes.** The author says where he lives, where he is from, who his boss is, how old he is, what he has not lived through. *"Tengo treinta y seis años. Mi memoria política comienza dentro de la Gran Moderación."* These details are not color; they are the author's standing to make the argument.

**Contrarian in the "hmm" sense.** The essays reframe (complaining is a public good; Peru's gridlock may be doing useful work) rather than attack. Provocation comes from the reframe, never from the tone.

**Respectful to everyone named.** Politicians are named when the argument is about institutions (Petro, Cepeda, López Obrador, Uribe, Orbán, Scalia all appear). Naming is fine; partisan framing is not. The test: a reader who voted for the person named should be able to finish the paragraph without feeling insulted, and should recognize the facts as fair. The one soft rule: criticize the practice, not the person's character.

**No slogans, no crusading.** The essays never say "freedom", "neoliberalism", "the left", "the right" as a verdict. *"Redistributive in costume"* was kept; *"socialist in costume"* was cut in revision. Keep cutting in that direction.

**Not a LinkedIn post, not a TED talk.** No "Here's the thing." No "Let that sink in." No numbered life lessons. The short note is allowed to be a little more LinkedIn, because it was one.

## 7. Rhythm and paragraphing

Measured on the published texts:

| | English essay | Spanish essay | Guideline |
|---|---|---|---|
| Words per paragraph | ~62 | ~58 | 50 to 70 |
| One-sentence paragraphs | 3 in 1,750 words | 7 in 2,800 words | at most 1 per 400 words |
| Hedging verbs (may/might/could; puede/podría) | 1 | 8 | fewer than 1 per 300 words |
| Rhetorical questions ending a section | 0 | 0 | at most 1 per essay |
| Section headers | 6 | 4 | 4 to 6 |

**Paragraphs are built.** A typical paragraph states a claim, gives the case or mechanism, and lands on a sentence that turns. Four to six sentences. The draft that averaged 26 words per paragraph read as a slide deck; the published essays do not.

**One-liners are reserved for the thesis and the close.** *"The frustration is the point."* *"La excepción fueron los últimos 30 años."* *"Losing possible. That is the key phrase."* They work because they are rare. Six in a row is a tic.

**Sentences average 16 to 17 words**, with deliberate short ones for emphasis. Vary length; do not write only short.

## 8. Signature devices (use each at most once per essay)

- **The parallel series of "must still matter" / "if X learn".** *"Congress must still matter when the president is popular. Courts must still matter when the majority is angry..."* / *"Si las empresas aprenden que... Si los jueces aprenden que... Si los legisladores aprenden que..."* Four or five beats, same grammatical frame, then a one-line landing.
- **The paired couplet that names both failure modes.** *"The failure mode of a speak-up culture is noise. The failure mode of a silence culture is catastrophe."* / *"Un país puede sufrir porque el ejecutivo tiene demasiado poder... o porque tiene demasiada poca estabilidad."*
- **The coined phrase, in italics or on its own line, paid off later.** *Love the gridlock* is introduced in §2 and cashed in the Peru section. *Separation of letterhead.* *Losing possible.*
- **The return.** The last section names the opening scene again and reads it differently in light of the argument.

---

## 9. Language

- **Spanish** for Latin American subjects or when the primary readers are Latin American. **English** for topics about economics at large or an international readership. Decide once; never switch mid-essay.
- **Spanish typography:** RAE spacing on percentages (*52 %*), comma decimals (*1,8*), period thousands (*3.500*), «en diez días» style numerals spelled out under ten. Use colons and commas where the English essays use em-dashes; Spanish prose tolerates far fewer dashes.
- **English typography:** em-dashes are part of the voice (28 in the English essay). Fine. Serial comma. Italics for titles and for the load-bearing phrase; bold only in the subtitle and for at most two or three definitional phrases per essay.
- **Acronyms:** expand on first use unless universally known to the target reader. CEPAL and OMS can stand alone in Spanish; UNFPA, CDC, SART, INAI, COFECE get a gloss.
- **Translating own work:** the Spanish version of "Who can stop whom?" tightened and localized (dropped the US section, added Colombian stakes). Translations are re-edits for the audience, not word-for-word.

## 10. Citations and Substack mechanics

- **Native Substack footnotes plus inline links. No reference list.** The published Spanish essay has 18 footnote markers and 15 inline links and no bibliography. Link the scholar's name or the institution in the body; put the full citation, URL, and any nuance in a footnote.
- **Footnotes are for two things:** the bibliographic record, and the "hay que matizar" counter-case that would break the body's rhythm.
- **Paste-ready markdown:** parentheses with URLs do not become links in Substack. Draft with `[text](url)` for inline links and `[^n]` for footnotes, then recreate the footnotes in the editor after pasting.
- **Previous-entry links:** "Como ya conté en otra entrada" gets a real link, filled before publishing.

---

## 11. Things to cut on sight

- **The "not X, it is Y" construction, in any form.** *"No es un principio: es una alternativa."* *"Demuestra algo más modesto."* *"That is not constitutionalism. That is constitutional opportunism."* *"No es un argumento. Lo que sí es un argumento es..."* Edgar's rule (2026-10-29): it reads as an AI tic, and it appeared six times in one machine-revised draft. Say what the thing is; if a contrast is needed, carry it inside one sentence with "pero" or "aunque", or let the example make it. Edgar's own "no solamente X; también Y" ("El problema no es simplemente que las personas quieran tener menos hijos. También existen restricciones...") is a different move, additive rather than a reveal, and stays. The published May essay used the reveal form once; going forward the rule is zero.
- A generalizing opener about society, modern life, or "what we want".
- "As an economist", "Soy economista y...", or any sentence whose job is to establish credentials.
- The textbook mini-lecture (comparative advantage explained in three paragraphs; "balanced harmony" of the branches). Keep the example, cut the lecture.
- A section that lists open research questions as the ending.
- Any one-liner that restates the previous sentence (*"No son preocupaciones menores."* after a paragraph listing the concerns).
- Stacked rhetorical questions.
- The title repeated as a section header.
- Hedges on claims that are not uncertain (*"no significa necesariamente"* where "no significa" is true).
- Decorative numbers.
- Country-ranking tourism ("in my home country we did it better").
- Hashtags, emojis, "thread", "let me explain", "here's the thing".

## 12. Pre-publication checklist

1. Subtitle states a thesis someone could disagree with.
2. First sentence puts the author in a scene.
3. The thesis is stated in plain words by the end of the opening section.
4. One named frame (scholar + work), not three.
5. Every case is real and does one job.
6. One full, honest concession to the other side.
7. Every number checked twice against the primary source; scope of each estimate stated.
8. Words per paragraph 50 to 70; one-liners ≤ 1 per 400 words; hedges ≤ 1 per 300 words.
9. At most one rhetorical question closing a section. Zero "not X, it is Y" sentences.
10. Last section returns to the opening scene and ends on one short line with personal stake.
11. Footnotes and inline links, no bibliography block; previous-entry links filled.
12. Spanish: RAE percentages, comma decimals, few dashes. English: em-dashes fine.
13. Read once as the person criticized would read it.
